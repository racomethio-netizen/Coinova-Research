Most software projects that go wrong do not fail because of the code. They fail because the team started building before agreeing on what "done" meant. A few days of careful scoping is the cheapest insurance a project can buy.

This is the approach we use before any engagement moves into design and engineering.

## Start with the objective, not the feature list

A feature list describes what a system *does*. An objective describes *why it matters*. "Build an invoicing module" is a feature. "Reduce the time between delivering work and getting paid" is an objective.

Objectives are useful because they let you say no. When a new idea appears halfway through a project, the question becomes simple: does it move the objective forward? If not, it can wait for a later release.

Good objectives are:

- **Specific** — tied to one process, team or customer group.
- **Observable** — you can tell whether things got better, even if you cannot measure it precisely on day one.
- **Owned** — someone on the client side cares about it and will judge the result.

## Describe the people, not just the users

"Users" is too abstract to design for. We write short descriptions of the actual people involved: what they do all day, which device they use, how good their connection is, what they are anxious about.

A dispatcher at a desk with a large monitor and a stable connection needs a very different interface from a driver checking a phone in direct sunlight. Both might be "users" of the same system.

## Write down the constraints early

Constraints shape architecture more than features do. The questions we ask in the first conversations include:

1. Which existing systems must this work with, and who controls them?
2. What data is sensitive, and which regulations apply to it?
3. Where will people use the software — and on what network?
4. What is the budget for running the system each month, not just for building it?
5. Who will maintain it after launch?

An honest answer to the last question often changes the technology choice entirely. A system your own team can maintain is worth more than a clever one they cannot.

## Find the smallest release worth launching

The first release should be small enough to ship early and complete enough to be genuinely useful to someone. Those two goals pull in opposite directions, and finding the balance is the core of scoping.

A useful exercise is to walk through one real scenario from start to finish — one order, one support request, one invoice — and list only the steps required for that scenario to work. Everything else is a candidate for a later release.

## Turn the scope into a written plan

At the end of scoping we produce a short document that everyone can read in ten minutes:

- the objective and how we will recognise progress,
- the people and scenarios the first release supports,
- the constraints and the decisions they led to,
- what is explicitly **out** of scope,
- milestones, with working software at each one.

The "out of scope" list is the most underrated part. It prevents misunderstandings far more effectively than a longer feature list.

## The payoff

Scoping does not slow a project down. It removes the expensive kind of uncertainty — the kind that is discovered after weeks of building in the wrong direction. When the plan is clear, engineering can move quickly and with confidence.
